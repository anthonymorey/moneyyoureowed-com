Why a “refund” claim is different from a “compensation” claim

This distinction is the whole reason a “claim firm’s cut” can sound reasonable on a bonus payout and predatory on a plain refund, and it’s worth being precise about it.

A refund, the subject of this guide, is your own money coming back to you: the price you already paid for a ticket the airline didn’t deliver as promised. Under federal rule, when you decline a significantly changed flight and any voucher or credit offered, the airline must give you a full refund of the airfare, taxes, and fees, in your original form of payment. In several situations that refund is automatic, meaning you don’t even have to ask. Source: 14 CFR § 260.6

“Compensation,” by contrast, usually refers to an extra payout on top of a refund, most familiar from EU/UK rules for flights departing the EU or on EU-based carriers, paid as an inconvenience payment separate from your ticket price. Claim firms built their business model chasing that extra money, since it genuinely didn’t exist until someone filed for it.

Here’s the part worth reading twice: at least one major claim firm, AirHelp, defines the amount its fee is calculated on broadly enough to cover both. Its own contract terms describe the fee as applying to “compensation, refund, damages, settlement, a gesture of goodwill, or otherwise.” In plain English, that means paying a firm to chase money that’s already legally yours, and that the airline may owe you automatically without a firm’s help at all, can still cost you a third of it or more. Source: AirHelp fee schedule, updated Apr. 24, 2026

What it costs to claim it yourself: $0

There is no filing fee, application fee, or processing fee for requesting your own refund from an airline. In several common situations, you don’t have to file anything: a refund becomes automatic when the flight is cancelled and no alternative is offered, when you reject the alternative or compensation the airline does offer, or when you simply don’t respond and the flight departs without you. Source: 14 CFR § 260.6(a)(2)

Where it isn’t automatic, the cost is your own time: comparing your original itinerary to what actually happened, and writing a request that cites the rule. That’s exactly what the checklist and templates below are for.

What claim firms actually charge, verified

Rather than repeat an unverified industry rumor about “up to 35%,” here’s the number pulled directly from a named firm’s own current pricing.

AirHelp, one of the best-known flight-claim firms, publishes a “No Win, No Fee” model: if it doesn’t recover anything, you owe nothing. If it does, its Standard/Service Fee is 35% (including VAT) of the total amount recovered. If the claim requires legal action, an additional 15% Legal Action Fee applies on top, for a combined cut of up to 50%. Source: AirHelp, Our Fees, updated Apr. 24, 2026

When paying a firm might still make sense

None of this means claim firms are a bad option in every case. There are situations where the trade genuinely favors paying someone else:

  • Your claim is contested, the airline is denying it, and you don’t want to escalate to legal action yourself.
  • Your trip involved multiple carriers or countries, and untangling who owes what is genuinely complicated.
  • You’re also pursuing EU/UK-style compensation on top of a refund, where a firm’s established relationships and legal-cost-fronting may speed things along.
  • You simply don’t have the time, and losing 35–50% of a recovery you wouldn’t otherwise have pursued at all still beats losing 100% of it.

What it shouldn’t take is a firm to get you a refund that was already automatic under federal rule, that’s the exact scenario where a percentage cut is hardest to justify.

Checklist: can you claim this yourself?

  • You have your original confirmation and the actual flight details to compare against it.
  • The change is clear-cut: cancelled outright, or a 3+ hour shift (6+ international), different airport, added connection, or downgrade.
  • You’re dealing with one airline, not a tangle of codeshare or multi-carrier responsibility.
  • You’re comfortable declining a voucher in writing and stating you want a cash refund instead.

If you checked all four, the free cheat-sheet above almost certainly covers what you need. If you didn’t, that’s exactly the profile where a claim firm’s fee starts to look more like a reasonable trade than a toll on your own money.

Frequently asked questions

Does it really cost nothing to claim my own airline refund?

Yes. Under federal rule, the airline owes you the refund directly, not a company you hire. In many situations (a cancelled flight with no alternative offered, or you rejecting what's offered) the refund must be issued automatically, without you filing anything at all. Where it isn't automatic, writing the request yourself costs nothing but your own time.

What exactly do claim firms like AirHelp charge?

Per AirHelp's own fee schedule, its Standard Fee is 35% (including VAT) of the total amount it recovers for you, which its own contract terms define broadly to include refunds, not only extra compensation. If the claim requires legal action, an additional 15% Legal Action Fee applies, for a combined cut of up to 50%.

Do claim firms’ fees apply even to a plain refund, or only to extra compensation?

For at least one major firm, AirHelp, the fee applies to both. Its contract terms define the amount its fee is calculated on as “compensation, refund, damages, settlement, a gesture of goodwill, or otherwise.” That means hiring a firm to recover money that was already legally yours, and that the airline may owe you automatically, can still cost you over a third of it.

When might paying a claim firm actually make sense?

When the claim is genuinely contested, spans multiple airlines or countries, or you simply don't have the time or patience to write the letter and follow up yourself. The trade is real: you give up a meaningful percentage in exchange for someone else handling the back-and-forth and, in some firms' models, fronting legal costs if the airline doesn't pay voluntarily.

Will using the free cheat-sheet get me paid faster than a claim firm would?

The refund timelines (7 business days for credit card, 20 calendar days for other payment methods) are set by federal rule and apply the same way regardless of who files the request. A claim firm doesn't speed up the airline's clock; it mainly saves you the effort of writing and following up on the request yourself, for a fee.

How Much Does It Cost to Get Your Deposit Back?

Three ways to fight a wrongly withheld security deposit: write the demand letter yourself, pay a kit to write it for you, or hire a lawyer. Here’s what each one actually costs, and the one honest catch about the lawyer option nobody mentions.

Last fact-checked: July 20, 2026

The short answer

Getting your deposit back costs $0 if you write your own demand letter, $29 if Deposit Defender writes it for you, or whatever a lawyer bills you, a number so variable it can’t be quoted honestly. The DIY path: $0

Nothing about demanding your own deposit back requires paying anyone. Find your state’s security-deposit statute. Read what it says about deadlines and permitted deductions. Then write a letter listing what was withheld, why the deduction is improper under that statute, and a deadline to pay you back. Send it by certified mail, so you can prove the landlord got it.

The honest trade-off is time and risk of missing something. State statutes are public and free to read, but they’re written in legal language, and a letter that doesn’t cite the right section or the right penalty carries less weight than one that does. If you’re comfortable with that kind of reading, this path costs you nothing but an evening.

Deposit Defender takes the same inputs (your state, your move-out date, your landlord’s deductions) and does the statute matching and the deduction-by-deduction wear-and-tear analysis for you, then generates a demand letter ready for certified mail. It doesn’t know anything a careful DIY reader couldn’t find themselves. This is the middle path: cheaper than a lawyer by a wide margin, faster than doing the research cold.

The lawyer path: variable, and often unnecessary

Here’s the part pricing pages for legal help usually skip: there is no honest single number for “what a lawyer costs.” It depends on the attorney, your state, whether they bill hourly or take the case on contingency, and how contested the dispute gets. What can be said with a straight face: certified lawyer-referral programs, like the State Bar of California’s Lawyer Referral Service, require the attorneys in their network to offer an initial consultation at a reduced fee or no fee at all. Source: State Bar of California, Lawyer Referral Service

There’s also a real upside worth knowing about before you rule a lawyer out entirely. A few states let you recover your own attorney’s fees from the landlord if you hire one and win: Massachusetts allows reasonable attorney’s fees on top of triple damages, and Colorado allows attorney fees and costs for willful violations. Why lawyers are rare in deposit disputes

Most withheld-deposit cases that end up in court end up in small claims court, precisely because it was designed for disputes people can handle themselves: no formal discovery, simplified rules, modest filing fees. California takes that design further than most. Its code of civil procedure says flatly that, with narrow exceptions, no attorney may take part in the conduct or defense of a small claims action. The exceptions are appearing for yourself, or for a law partnership or professional corporation made up entirely of attorneys, which isn’t the situation most renters are in. Source: Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 116.530

Put plainly: if your case is the kind of deposit dispute this guide is about, and it’s heard in small claims, paying a lawyer to argue it for you may not even be an option on the table. That’s the detail that makes the DIY-vs-kit comparison the one that actually matters for most renters. The lawyer question only becomes real once a case is big enough, or contested enough, to leave small claims altogether.

Which one should you actually pick

Run through this in order. Stop at the first one that fits.

  • Your deposit is a normal amount (a month or two’s rent), the deductions are the usual suspects (cleaning, paint, carpet), and you’re comfortable reading your state’s statute yourself: DIY, $0.
  • Same situation, but you’d rather not spend an evening parsing statute language, or you want the wear-and-tear analysis double-checked against your specific deductions: Deposit Defender, $29.
  • The amount is unusually large, the landlord has already lawyered up, the case involves something beyond a straightforward deposit statute (a countersuit, a lease dispute, property damage claims), or it’s left small claims court entirely: talk to a lawyer, starting with a certified referral service for the reduced-fee consult.

Nothing stops you from starting cheap and escalating. Plenty of renters send the $0 or $29 letter first; a chunk of landlords pay rather than risk a court case where the burden of proof, in several states, sits on them. Only the ones who don’t pay ever require the next step.

Frequently asked questions

Is it really free to demand my deposit back myself?

Yes. Reading your state’s security-deposit statute, writing a demand letter, and sending it by certified mail cost nothing but your time and the price of a stamp. Filing in small claims court afterward involves a modest filing fee, but not a lawyer’s bill.

What does Deposit Defender actually do for the $29?

It matches your state to its security-deposit statute, checks your specific deductions against the wear-and-tear standard, and generates a demand letter citing the statute, ready for certified mail. It replaces roughly an evening of research and drafting with about fifteen minutes.

Can I hire a lawyer for a security deposit dispute?

You can consult one, and certified referral services require a reduced-fee or free first consultation. But if your case ends up in small claims court, California generally doesn’t allow attorneys to represent parties there at all, which is a large part of why most deposit disputes never involve one.

If a lawyer can’t appear in small claims court, what’s the point of hiring one?

Consultation and strategy, mostly: reviewing your evidence, telling you whether your case is strong, and helping if the dispute grows beyond small claims (a countersuit, a larger claim, an appeal). For a standard withheld-deposit letter and small claims filing, most renters never need to get there.

Can I get my attorney’s fees back if I win?

In some states, yes. Massachusetts and Colorado both let a tenant who wins recover reasonable attorney’s fees from the landlord in addition to the statutory damages. Most states without that provision leave each side to cover their own legal costs.

Is the $29 kit cheaper than just being wrong and losing the case?

That’s the comparison that matters more than DIY-vs-kit, honestly. A demand letter that cites the wrong statute or misses your state’s deadline is weaker evidence than one that gets the details right, whichever way you produce it. The $29 buys certainty that the statute lookup and deduction analysis were done correctly; it doesn’t buy anything a careful DIY letter couldn’t also achieve.

Does filing in small claims court cost money?

Filing fees are modest and vary by state and by the amount you’re claiming, but they’re a small claims court fee, not a lawyer’s retainer. Check your local court’s fee schedule for the exact number where you live.

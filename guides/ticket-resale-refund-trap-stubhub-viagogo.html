StubHub or Viagogo Won’t Refund You? Your Real Options

Cancelled events, seats that turned out to be nowhere near the ones on the map, claims that sit unanswered for a month. Here’s the honest order of who to push, and which door actually gets your money back.

Last fact-checked: July 21, 2026

The short answer

You have three doors, and they’re not equal. The marketplace’s own guarantee is the slowest and least reliable. A written credit-card dispute under the Fair Credit Billing Act is the one that reliably moves, and you generally have only 60 days from the statement date to file it. Your state attorney general is the third door, and it’s more powerful in numbers than most people think: a group of attorneys general is exactly what forced StubHub to pay cash instead of 120% credit for pandemic-cancelled events. Source: D.C. Office of the Attorney General

Your event was cancelled: cash vs. credit

When an event is cancelled outright, this is your strongest position, and it’s worth knowing the history before you accept whatever the marketplace offers first. In March 2020, as events shut down, StubHub stopped honoring cash refunds and told buyers they’d get account credit worth 120% of their purchase instead. Ten states and the District of Columbia opened a coordinated investigation. StubHub reversed course, and in a settlement announced on September 14, 2021 it agreed to pay cash refunds to eligible buyers who had purchased before March 25, 2020 for events that were later cancelled. Source: D.C. Office of the Attorney General

The honest read: that settlement was tied to the pandemic and doesn’t automatically entitle you to cash for a show cancelled today. But it demolishes the idea that “store credit is all we can do” is a fixed rule. If you want cash and the event genuinely didn’t happen, ask for it in writing, decline the credit explicitly, and be ready to escalate.

The seat wasn’t what the map showed

This is the harder case, and I won’t pretend otherwise. If you paid for a listing that showed one section and ended up somewhere materially different, or the stage turned out to be nowhere near where the seating map implied, you’re in a gray zone. A dispute purely about quality or a wrong impression is weaker than a dispute over tickets you never received at all.

What makes the difference is evidence and framing. Screenshot the original listing the moment you notice a problem, before it disappears. Then frame your complaint not as “I didn’t like my seats” but as “the tickets were not delivered as described in the listing I paid for.” That’s a materially different claim, and it’s the version your card issuer can act on. Marketplaces will often argue a seating map is only illustrative; your job is to show the listing made a specific promise and broke it.

The credit-card dispute: your reliable lever

If you paid by credit card, this is the door most people underuse. The Fair Credit Billing Act treats a charge for something you ordered and never received, or that wasn’t delivered as agreed, as a billing error you can dispute. You generally have 60 days from the date the first statement showing the charge was sent to file that dispute in writing. Once you do, the card issuer must acknowledge it within 30 days and resolve it within 90 days. One limit worth knowing: a dispute purely about the quality of an item isn’t treated as a billing error. Source: Federal Trade Commission, consumer.ftc.gov

The practical mistake I see people make is waiting. They let the marketplace’s claim process crawl on for weeks, and by the time it’s clearly going nowhere, the 60-day window is closing. Don’t serialize these. Open the marketplace claim and, if it stalls, start the written card dispute in parallel so the clock never runs out on your best option.

Why the fees felt like a trap, and what changed in 2025

Part of the reason resale tickets feel like a bad deal is that the price you agreed to often wasn’t the price you saw first. Regulators have named this. On July 31, 2024, the District of Columbia Attorney General sued StubHub, alleging that since 2015 it used “drip pricing” to advertise a deceptively low number and hide mandatory fulfillment and service fees until late in checkout, at an estimated cost of about $118 million to District consumers. Source: D.C. Office of the Attorney General

The bigger shift is a federal one. The FTC’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees took effect on May 12, 2025. Live-event ticket sellers that advertise a price now have to show the total you’ll pay, including mandatory fees, and display that total more prominently than any other pricing except the final payment amount. Source: Federal Trade Commission

Why this matters to you: if a site advertised one number and only sprang mandatory fees on you at the end after May 12, 2025, that’s the precise behavior the rule prohibits. It won’t auto-refund you, but it gives your attorney general complaint real teeth.

What to do, in order

Work down this list. Don’t skip a step, but don’t wait for one to fully finish before starting the next when your 60-day card window is ticking.

  • Document everything now. Screenshot the listing, the price you were shown, the confirmation, and every message. Evidence gathered late is evidence you may not have.
  • File the marketplace claim in writing. State plainly whether the event was cancelled, the tickets never arrived, or they weren’t as described, and say clearly that you want a cash refund, not credit.
  • Open a credit-card dispute in parallel if it stalls. Put it in writing within 60 days of the statement. This is the door with a neutral referee and a legal timeline.
  • File with your state attorney general. It’s free, and collective complaints are what have moved these companies before. Mention hidden fees or a denied refund specifically.

The unglamorous truth is that the buyer who wins is usually the one who was organized and fast, not the one with the angriest email. A calm paper trail beats a furious phone call every time.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a cash refund from StubHub if my event is cancelled?

Sometimes, and there is precedent for it. When StubHub switched to offering 120% account credit instead of cash for events cancelled early in the pandemic, ten states and the District of Columbia investigated, and in a September 14, 2021 settlement StubHub agreed to pay cash refunds to eligible buyers who bought before March 25, 2020. That settlement does not automatically cover a concert cancelled today, but it shows a credit-only answer is not the final word. Ask for cash in writing, and if the marketplace refuses, take it to your card issuer and your state attorney general.

Viagogo says the seating map was only illustrative. Can I still get my money back?

This is the hardest case, because a dispute purely about quality or a wrong impression is not the same as a charge for something you never received. Your strongest ground is to show the listing promised a specific section or seats and delivered something materially different, then argue the tickets were not delivered as agreed. Keep the original listing screenshot, put the discrepancy in writing to the marketplace, and if it stonewalls, raise the same not-as-agreed argument with your card issuer.

How long do I have to dispute a ticket charge with my credit card?

Under the Fair Credit Billing Act you generally have 60 days from the date the first statement showing the charge was sent to dispute it in writing. Once you do, the issuer must acknowledge your dispute within 30 days and resolve it within 90 days. Do not wait for the marketplace to finish its own slow process before you start the clock with your card issuer.

Do resale sites have to show all their fees upfront now?

Yes. The FTC’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees took effect on May 12, 2025, and live-event ticket sellers that advertise a price must now show the total price you will pay, including mandatory fees, and display that total more prominently than other pricing except the final payment amount. If a resale site advertised one number and only revealed mandatory fees deep in checkout, that is the exact practice the rule was written to stop.

Is it worth filing a complaint with my state attorney general?

Often yes, especially when a marketplace ignores your dispute for weeks. The 2021 StubHub cash-refund settlement happened because attorneys general in eleven jurisdictions acted together after consumer complaints. One complaint rarely moves a company on its own, but it feeds the pattern regulators look for, and it costs you nothing but a few minutes.

What if the marketplace only offers me credit, not cash?

Credit is a choice they are offering you, not always the limit of what you are owed. If you would rather have cash, say so in writing and decline the credit explicitly. StubHub’s own history shows a company can start with credit-only and later be required to pay cash. If they hold firm, a credit-card dispute is the lever that puts the money question in front of a neutral third party.

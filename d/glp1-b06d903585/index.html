Start Here — Which denial do you have?

⚡ First branch: are you on MEDICARE?

If yes, stop — before writing any appeal, check the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge (see the "Medicare GLP-1 Bridge" guide in this kit). It's a real CMS pilot running July 2026 – December 2027: a flat $50/month for Wegovy, Foundayo, or Zepbound KwikPen if you meet the clinical criteria and aren't already getting a GLP-1 through Part D. For many Medicare readers, enrolling there is faster and cheaper than months of appeals — we'd rather point you to the $50 route than sell you a fight you don't need. If you don't qualify (or your diagnosis is diabetes/sleep apnea/fatty liver, which route through normal Part D), come back here and use the Part D appeal path.

If you have commercial insurance (through work or the marketplace), continue below — this kit's appeal paths are built for you.

Your denial letter tells you what the insurer says is missing, excluded, or unsupported. Start with the exact reason in that letter—not the explanation you received at the pharmacy or by phone.

The goal is to identify:

  1. What decision was actually made
  2. Why the claim or authorization was denied
  3. Which deadline applies
  4. Which template and escalation path to use

If the letter gives more than one reason, answer every reason in the same appeal.

Before you choose a path

Have these documents in front of you:

  • The denial letter or electronic coverage decision
  • The insurance card for the plan that denied coverage
  • The prescription and prescribed dose
  • The drug’s name: Wegovy, Zepbound, Ozempic, or another GLP-1 medication
  • The condition being treated
  • Your plan’s formulary or covered-drug list
  • The plan’s prior-authorization criteria, if available
  • Your Summary Plan Description, Evidence of Coverage, or Certificate of Coverage
  • Notes showing medications, programs, or treatments you previously tried
  • Relevant chart notes, lab results, BMI history, and diagnoses
  • The date you received the denial

Do not assume the appeal deadline starts when you opened the letter. Use the deadline printed in the denial notice or governing plan document.

No written denial yet? Ask the insurer for the decision in writing, including the reason, criteria used, deadline, appeal address or portal, and instructions for requesting the records relied on. A pharmacy rejection message may not be the plan’s formal appealable decision.

The two-minute decision map

Step 1: Was a formal coverage decision made?

A. The pharmacy says “prior authorization needed,” but no prior authorization was submitted

This may be a request-stage problem rather than an appeal.

Use:

  • Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet
  • Insurer Playbook for your insurer
  • Denial-Letter Decoder if you have a pharmacy rejection code

Ask the prescriber to submit the prior authorization with the clinical records listed in the worksheet.

Do not label the first submission as an appeal unless the insurer has already denied a prior-authorization request.

B. A prior authorization, claim, formulary exception, or coverage request was denied

Continue to Step 2.

C. Your employer announced that GLP-1 or weight-loss coverage is ending

Go to Path 3: Plan exclusion or benefit removed.

An employer’s decision to remove a benefit is different from an insurer deciding that you failed medical criteria. An appeal generally cannot force a plan to add a benefit that its governing documents clearly exclude. You may still have a case if:

  • The exclusion does not apply to your diagnosis
  • The plan treated a diabetes claim as a weight-loss claim
  • The plan documents conflict
  • The change was applied before its effective date
  • Required notice was not provided
  • The plan failed to apply an exception or transition-of-care provision that actually exists in its terms

Path 1 — Prior authorization required or incomplete

Choose this path when

The denial says:

  • No prior authorization was submitted
  • The insurer did not receive the requested records
  • The request lacked a diagnosis, BMI history, lab result, treatment history, or prescriber response
  • The authorization expired
  • The request went to the wrong benefit manager or review channel
  • The prescriber submitted the request after the medication was dispensed

Use

  1. Letter 1 — Prior Authorization Required or Incomplete
  2. Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet
  3. The matching Insurer Playbook

Attach

  • A copy of the denial
  • Completed authorization form, if the plan requires one
  • Current prescription and dose
  • Diagnosis and relevant diagnosis code
  • BMI history and weight-related comorbidities when relevant
  • A1C or diabetes records when relevant
  • Prior medications, dates, outcomes, and adverse effects
  • Relevant chart notes
  • Any insurer-specific criteria checklist

Ask before filing

Call the number on the denial and ask:

“Is this a denial of a completed prior-authorization request, or is the request still open because information is missing?”

Then ask:

“What exact information is missing, where must it be sent, and will a completed submission be treated as a new request or an appeal?”

Write down the representative’s name, call reference number, date, and answer.

Watch for

“Records not received” does not necessarily mean the prescriber failed to send them. Confirm the fax number, portal, case number, and receipt status.

Path 2 — Not medically necessary or clinical criteria not met

Choose this path when

The denial says:

  • You do not meet the plan’s BMI threshold
  • A required comorbidity was not documented
  • The records do not show participation in a required program
  • The records do not show enough improvement for continued coverage
  • The requested drug is not medically necessary
  • The diagnosis does not support the requested medication
  • The plan calls the use experimental, investigational, or off-label
  • Continuation or renewal criteria were not met

Use

  1. Letter 2 — Medical Necessity or Clinical-Criteria Denial
  2. Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet
  3. Escalation Ladder if the internal appeal is denied

Attach

  • The denial letter
  • The plan’s cited clinical policy or criteria
  • A clinician’s letter addressing each disputed criterion
  • Relevant chart notes and diagnosis history
  • Weight and BMI history, not just today’s measurement
  • Comorbidity documentation
  • A1C and diabetes treatment history when relevant
  • Previous treatments, outcomes, contraindications, and adverse effects
  • Evidence of response if requesting continuation

Get the rule the insurer used

The denial should identify or explain the basis for the decision. Ask for:

  • The complete clinical criteria in effect on the decision date
  • The medical policy version and effective date
  • Records reviewed
  • Reviewer credentials or specialty, if disclosed
  • Internal notes and reports allowed under your plan’s claims procedure
  • The specific criterion the insurer says was not met

For many employer plans governed by ERISA, federal claims-procedure rules require specified information about adverse benefit determinations and access to relevant claim documents. See 29 C.F.R. § 2560.503-1. Whether those rules apply depends on the plan.

Watch for

Do not argue only that the medication would help. Connect the records to the plan’s stated criterion, then explain why the criterion is met, misapplied, or should be excepted.

Path 3 — Plan exclusion or weight-loss benefit removed

Choose this path when

The denial says:

  • Weight-loss medications are excluded
  • Anti-obesity drugs are not a covered benefit
  • The drug class is excluded regardless of medical necessity
  • Your employer elected not to cover the benefit
  • Coverage ended on a stated date
  • The drug is covered for diabetes but not for weight management

Use

  1. Letter 3 — Plan Exclusion or Weight-Loss Benefit Denial
  2. The plan-document section of the Escalation Ladder
  3. Your Insurer Playbook
  4. The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge Program One-Pager if you have Medicare and may qualify

First, identify what the plan actually excludes

Request:

  • The current Summary Plan Description, Evidence of Coverage, or Certificate of Coverage
  • The prescription-drug formulary
  • Drug-specific coverage criteria
  • Amendments or notices changing GLP-1 coverage
  • The effective date of the exclusion
  • Any exception, transition, continuation, or grandfathering terms
  • The document showing whether the employer plan is self-funded or fully insured

Use Letter 3 when you can argue that

  • The exclusion does not apply to the diagnosis being treated
  • The request was coded under the wrong diagnosis
  • The plan covers the drug for one condition and your records support that condition
  • The plan documents and denial conflict
  • The plan applied an exclusion before its effective date
  • The plan failed to follow an exception written into its own terms
  • The drug was treated as a weight-loss request when it was prescribed for another covered indication

Set expectations

An appeal is less likely to change a clear, properly applied benefit exclusion than a missing-document or clinical-criteria denial. External reviewers generally decide whether the plan correctly applied its coverage terms and applicable law; they do not simply rewrite an employer’s benefit design.

If the exclusion clearly applies, use the Cost-Bridge Guide while you check:

  • Whether another covered medication is clinically appropriate
  • Whether the employer offers an exception process
  • Whether coverage changes at the next plan year
  • Whether a manufacturer program is available and lawful for your coverage type
  • Whether another source of coverage is available

Path 4 — Step therapy, preferred drug, dose, or quantity limit

Choose this path when

The denial says:

  • You must try another drug first
  • You must fail one or more preferred medications
  • A preferred GLP-1 must be used before the requested drug
  • The prescribed quantity exceeds the plan limit
  • The dose is outside the plan’s rule
  • A refill was requested too early
  • The requested titration schedule was rejected

Use

  1. Letter 4 — Step-Therapy or Quantity-Limit Exception
  2. Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet
  3. Your Insurer Playbook

Attach

For step therapy:

  • Names of previously tried medications
  • Approximate start and stop dates
  • Dose, if known
  • Treatment outcome
  • Adverse effects
  • Contraindications or interactions
  • Reason a preferred alternative is not clinically appropriate

For quantity or dose limits:

  • Current prescription
  • Titration history
  • Relevant dispensing history
  • Clinical reason for the requested dose or quantity
  • Explanation of any replacement needed after a device failure, loss, or dose change
  • The plan’s quantity-limit criteria

Ask the insurer

“Is this a step-therapy denial, a formulary denial, a quantity-limit denial, or a claim-timing rejection?”

Then ask:

“What exception form applies, and what facts must the prescriber document?”

Watch for

A “refill too soon” rejection can be caused by claim timing rather than a coverage denial. Ask the pharmacy when the next covered fill is available before starting an appeal.

Path 5 — Compounded-version denial

Choose this path when

The denial says:

  • Compounded drugs are excluded
  • The product is not FDA approved
  • The ingredient or dosage form is not covered
  • The compound is not on the formulary
  • The pharmacy or compound is ineligible under the plan
  • The plan will cover an FDA-approved product but not the compounded version

Use

  1. Letter 5 — Compounded-Version Denial
  2. Cost-Bridge Guide
  3. Your plan’s compound-drug benefit rules

First, confirm exactly what was prescribed

Ask the prescriber and pharmacy for:

  • The complete name and formulation
  • Whether the prescription is for a compounded product
  • Ingredients and dosage form
  • Dispensing pharmacy
  • The clinical reason the compounded version was requested
  • Whether an FDA-approved product is available and clinically appropriate
  • Whether the claim was submitted under the correct pharmacy-benefit procedure

FDA states that compounded drugs are not FDA approved and that FDA does not verify their safety, effectiveness, or quality before marketing. Compounding may be appropriate for an identified patient in certain circumstances, but compounded products should not be described as FDA-approved equivalents. See FDA, “Understanding the Risks of Compounded Drugs.”

Set expectations

Many plans exclude compounded products. Medical preference alone may not override a clear exclusion.

Letter 5 is most useful when:

  • The plan offers compound coverage but the claim was classified incorrectly
  • The plan’s stated exception criteria are met
  • The requested dosage form is medically necessary and supported by the prescriber
  • An ingredient, pharmacy, or billing error caused the denial
  • The insurer overlooked a benefit provision that applies

Do not claim that a compounded product is identical to, interchangeable with, or approved in the same way as an FDA-approved drug unless reliable product-specific authority supports that statement.

Special routing: the denial is not mainly about medical criteria

Eligibility or terminated coverage

If the denial says you were not enrolled or coverage had ended:

  1. Contact the employer benefits office, Marketplace, Medicare, Medicaid agency, or plan enrollment unit.
  2. Request the effective dates and enrollment record in writing.
  3. Provide premium-payment or enrollment evidence.
  4. Ask whether claims can be reprocessed after eligibility is corrected.

Use an appeal letter only if the plan maintains that its eligibility decision is correct.

Coordination of benefits

If the plan says another insurer is primary:

  1. Ask which other coverage appears in the insurer’s records.
  2. Update coordination-of-benefits information.
  3. Ask the pharmacy to resubmit the claim.
  4. Appeal only if the plan refuses to correct an inaccurate record or denies after correction.

Pharmacy or billing error

If the rejection mentions an invalid code, prescriber identifier, days’ supply, quantity, or product number:

  1. Ask the pharmacy to identify the rejected field.
  2. Correct and resubmit the claim.
  3. Request a formal coverage decision if the corrected claim is still denied.

Ozempic requested for weight loss

Ozempic’s FDA-approved indication includes treatment of type 2 diabetes and specified cardiovascular or kidney-risk uses in certain adults with type 2 diabetes; it is not FDA approved specifically for chronic weight management. Wegovy and Zepbound have separate FDA-approved indications.

If the denial is based on diagnosis or use:

  • Do not change or add a diagnosis that is not supported by the medical record.
  • Use Letter 2 if the prescriber is requesting an exception based on medical necessity.
  • Use Letter 3 if the plan excludes treatment for weight management.
  • Ask the prescriber whether an on-label covered alternative is appropriate.

Choose the right escalation system

The name on the insurance card does not always tell you which law or regulator controls the plan.

Individual or Marketplace plan

Start with the plan’s internal appeal instructions. Qualifying denials may then be eligible for external review under federal or state rules. ACA external-review standards are addressed in 45 C.F.R. § 147.136, subject to plan type and other exceptions.

Use: Internal appeal → external review → state Department of Insurance complaint, as applicable.

Employer plan: fully insured

The insurer administers coverage under a policy purchased by the employer. State insurance rules generally apply alongside any applicable federal claims requirements.

Use: Internal appeal → external review → state Department of Insurance complaint, as applicable.

Ask HR or the plan administrator to confirm in writing that the plan is fully insured.

Employer plan: self-funded or self-insured

The employer pays claims, even if an insurer such as UHC, Aetna, Cigna, or a BCBS company administers them. State insurance departments often have limited authority over self-funded ERISA plans.

Use: Plan’s internal appeal → applicable external-review process → employer plan administrator and U.S. Department of Labor Employee Benefits Security Administration, when appropriate.

Ask:

“Is this plan fully insured or self-funded, and who is the named plan administrator?”

Do not rely only on the logo on the card.

Medicare Part D

Medicare drug appeals use the Medicare coverage-determination and appeal system, not the standard ACA Marketplace appeal path.

Use:

  • Your Part D plan’s coverage-determination or redetermination instructions
  • The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge Program One-Pager
  • Medicare’s official appeal guidance

If the issue is a formulary restriction, ask whether the prescriber must submit a supporting statement for an exception.

Medicaid

Medicaid appeals and fair-hearing procedures vary by state and managed-care arrangement.

Use:

  • The instructions in the denial
  • Your state Medicaid agency or managed-care plan process
  • State legal-aid or health-consumer assistance if needed

Do not use a commercial-plan deadline unless the Medicaid notice says it applies.

If you have more than one denial reason

Insurers may combine reasons. For example:

  • “Prior authorization required” and “step therapy not completed”
  • “Not medically necessary” and “records were insufficient”
  • “Nonformulary” and “preferred alternatives not tried”
  • “Weight-loss benefit excluded” and “diagnosis does not meet criteria”

Use one lead template and add the relevant section from the second template.

Lead-template order

  1. Plan exclusion if the plan says the benefit is not covered at all
  2. Medical necessity if the insurer reviewed clinical criteria
  3. Step therapy or quantity limit if a specific restriction controls
  4. Prior authorization incomplete if documents or forms are missing
  5. Compounded-version denial if the product’s compounded status controls

State each denial reason in a separate heading and answer it with separate evidence.

“I am calling about denial number [CASE NUMBER] for [DRUG AND DOSE]. Please read me every reason for the denial and identify the plan provision, formulary rule, or clinical criterion used.”

“Is the next step a corrected claim, a new prior-authorization request, an internal appeal, a formulary exception, or another process?”

“Please tell me whether expedited review is available and what standard applies.”

“Please give me the call reference number and confirm how I can obtain this information in writing.”

If waiting could seriously harm your health

Ask the prescriber and insurer whether expedited review is appropriate. Expedited review is generally reserved for circumstances in which the ordinary timeline could seriously jeopardize life or health, the ability to regain maximum function, or—in some systems—cause severe pain that cannot be adequately managed without the requested care. The exact standard and timeline depend on the coverage type and appeal level.

Do not describe a request as urgent unless the facts support it. Ask the treating clinician to explain the medical urgency directly.

If you believe you are experiencing a medical emergency, seek emergency care. Do not wait for an insurance appeal.

What to do next

  1. Circle the exact denial language.
  2. Select the matching path above.
  3. Complete the named letter template.
  4. Give the Prior-Auth Worksheet to the prescriber if clinical information is involved.
  5. Use the insurer playbook for filing instructions.
  6. Keep proof of submission.
  7. If denied again, move to the Escalation Ladder without missing the next deadline.
  8. Use the Cost-Bridge Guide only as a temporary access option—not as a substitute for preserving appeal rights.

The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge — Your $50/Month Option (Verified)

If you're on Medicare, read this BEFORE writing any appeal. For many Medicare beneficiaries, the fastest path to a covered GLP-1 isn't an appeal at all — it's a temporary CMS pilot program that started July 1, 2026. This page is verified against CMS's own program page and the official Medicare fact sheet (CMS Product No. 12234, June 2026).

What it is — the facts

Question Verified answer
Official name Medicare GLP-1 Bridge — a CMS demonstration (pilot) program
Dates July 1, 2026 – December 31, 2027 (temporary; could change or end after that)
Cost Flat $50/month copay, regardless of income
Cost fine print Does not count toward your Part D deductible or out-of-pocket maximum; the low-income subsidy (Extra Help) does not apply; can't be paid through the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan
Covered drugs Foundayo® (tablet), Wegovy® (injection or tablet), and Zepbound® KwikPen® ONLY. The Zepbound single-dose pen and vials are excluded. Ozempic and compounded semaglutide/tirzepatide are NOT covered.
Where to read it medicare.gov/coverage/weight-loss-drugs · cms.gov → search "Medicare GLP-1 Bridge" · the fact sheet PDF: medicare.gov/publications (Product No. 12234)

Important framing: this is a temporary pilot run under CMS demonstration authority — Medicare's underlying rule excluding weight-loss drugs from Part D has NOT changed. The Bridge operates around that exclusion until the end of 2027. Do not treat it as a permanent benefit.

Who qualifies (ALL four must be true)

  1. You have Medicare Part D drug coverage (stand-alone plan or Medicare Advantage with drug coverage — but NOT a Private Fee-for-Service plan, cost plan, or PACE).
  2. You are NOT already getting a GLP-1 through your Part D plan.
  3. You do NOT have type 2 diabetes, moderate-to-severe sleep apnea, or fatty liver disease. (If you do, stop here — your GLP-1 should be covered through your regular Part D plan for that diagnosis. Use the appeal letters in this kit if your plan denied it.)
  4. You meet the clinical bar (age 18+, and one of):
  5. BMI 35 or higher, or
  6. BMI 30+ plus certain heart failure, uncontrolled high blood pressure, or chronic kidney disease (stage 3a+), or
  7. BMI 27+ plus prediabetes or a prior heart attack, stroke, or peripheral artery disease.

Decision guide

  • Medicare + qualify for the Bridge + want a covered drug (Wegovy, Foundayo, or Zepbound KwikPen) → enroll in the Bridge. $50/month beats months of appeals. Ask your prescriber to note your qualifying BMI/conditions.
  • Medicare + your diagnosis is diabetes / sleep apnea / fatty liver → the Bridge isn't for you, and it doesn't need to be: your drug should run through normal Part D. If your plan denied it, that's a standard Part D coverage determination + appeal — use the Part D path below.
  • Medicare + want a drug the Bridge doesn't cover (e.g., Ozempic for weight loss) → the Bridge won't help; a weight-loss-only Ozempic claim also faces Part D's weight-loss exclusion. Talk to your prescriber about whether a covered alternative fits.

If you need the Part D appeal path instead

For diabetes/covered-indication denials: ask your plan for a coverage determination (the plan generally must answer within 72 hours for a standard request or 24 hours for an expedited one). If denied, the first appeal is a redetermination by your plan, then a reconsideration by an independent review entity. Every denial notice states its own deadline — the notice governs. Free unbiased help: your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) — locator at shiphelp.org.

Watch-outs

  • Manufacturer savings cards cannot be combined with Medicare (federal anti-kickback rules) — the Bridge's $50/month replaces that route for covered drugs.
  • The Bridge runs through your pharmacy like a normal prescription once you're enrolled; if a pharmacy claim rejects, ask whether the claim was routed to the Bridge program rather than your Part D plan.

GLP-1 Appeal Letter Pack

Five fill-in templates for five common denial reasons:

  1. Prior authorization required or incomplete
  2. Not medically necessary
  3. Weight-loss drug or benefit exclusion
  4. Step-therapy or quantity limit
  5. Compounded-version denial

Use the template that matches the reason stated in your denial, Explanation of Benefits, portal message, or pharmacy rejection. Copy the insurer’s wording exactly. If the notice gives more than one reason, address each reason separately or combine the relevant sections.

Before you send an appeal

  • Read the denial notice first. Use the filing address, portal, fax number, deadline, and appeal level shown on that notice.
  • Do not guess at facts. Replace every {FILL-IN} field with accurate information. Delete anything that does not apply.
  • Include the denial notice. Mark the exact denial reason, date, claim or authorization number, and service or medication at issue.
  • Use your plan’s own criteria. Request the medical policy, pharmacy policy, formulary rule, clinical criteria, and plan provision used to make the decision.
  • Ask the prescriber to provide clinical support. A consumer appeal can preserve your rights and organize the record, but the strongest clinical statements generally come from the treating clinician.
  • Keep proof of submission. Save the complete appeal, attachments, portal confirmation, fax confirmation, certified-mail receipt, and notes from every call.
  • Ask about an expedited appeal if delay could seriously jeopardize your life, health, or ability to regain maximum function. The plan—not this kit—decides whether the request qualifies. Federal appeal rules and exact timelines vary by plan type. See the U.S. Department of Labor’s claims-procedure guidance and HealthCare.gov’s appeal overview.[^1][^2]
  • Do not miss the deadline while waiting for records. If necessary, submit a short appeal before the deadline and state that supporting records will follow, if the plan permits supplementation.

Important: An appeal does not guarantee coverage. A plan may lawfully exclude a drug or benefit, apply medical-necessity criteria, require step therapy, or decline coverage of a compounded product. The purpose of an appeal is to correct errors, document how you meet applicable criteria, and require the plan to explain its decision.

Insurer Playbooks: UHC, Aetna, Cigna, BCBS, Kaiser and Humana

Use the insurer name on your card to find the right section. Then check the denial notice and plan documents before filing. The logo alone does not determine your appeal rules.

A UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, Blue Cross Blue Shield, Kaiser or Humana card can represent:

  • An employer-sponsored, self-funded plan governed primarily by federal ERISA rules.
  • An employer-sponsored, fully insured plan regulated by a state.
  • An individual or Marketplace plan.
  • Medicare Advantage or Medicare Part D.
  • Medicaid or a state-administered program.
  • A grandfathered health plan with different appeal rights.

Your denial notice should identify the legal plan or insurance company, the reason for denial, the filing deadline and the address, fax number or portal used for an appeal. If this playbook conflicts with that notice, follow the notice and ask the insurer to confirm the instructions in writing.

Before You Call Any Insurer

Have these items in front of you:

  • Member ID and group number.
  • Denial notice, including its date and reference or case number.
  • Drug name, dose and quantity.
  • Prescriber’s name and contact information.
  • The diagnosis submitted with the request.
  • Whether the prescription is for an FDA-approved indication or an off-label use.
  • The date treatment is supposed to begin or continue.
  • Any prior medications, lifestyle-program requirements or step-therapy history.
  • Relevant BMI history, comorbidities, A1C results or other clinical records.
  • A pen and a call log.

Ask the representative to identify your plan type before discussing the denial:

Is this plan fully insured, self-funded through an employer, Marketplace, Medicare Advantage, Part D or Medicaid? What legal entity made the denial?

Also ask whether the drug was denied under the pharmacy benefit, medical benefit or a plan exclusion. That determines which department and appeal process apply.

Deadline Rules to Check

Do not assume the following federal timeframes apply to your plan. Confirm the deadline printed on your denial notice.

  • Many non-grandfathered commercial plans must allow at least 180 days after an adverse benefit determination to request an internal appeal. See 45 C.F.R. § 147.136 and the claims-and-appeals rules it incorporates.
  • The federal external-review process generally uses a four-month filing period after receipt of a final internal adverse benefit determination, but state processes and eligible-plan rules can differ. See 45 C.F.R. § 147.136 and CMS, “External Appeals”.
  • Urgent-care claims can have shorter decision deadlines, and an expedited external review may sometimes proceed while an expedited internal appeal is underway. Eligibility depends on the medical circumstances and plan type.
  • Medicare drug appeals use different deadlines and terminology. A redetermination request is generally due within 60 calendar days of the coverage-denial notice, subject to good-cause extensions.
  • Medicaid appeal deadlines vary by state and program.

File early. Keep proof of submission. If you file through a portal, save the confirmation page as a PDF or screenshot.

Escalation Ladder

From insurer denial to internal appeal, independent external review, and regulator complaint

Use this guide when a health plan or pharmacy-benefit manager denies coverage for a GLP-1 medication.

The basic path:

  1. Read the denial notice and collect the claim file.
  2. File the internal appeal before the notice’s deadline.
  3. If the denial is upheld, request external review if the dispute qualifies.
  4. File a regulator complaint if the plan mishandled the claim, appeal, or external-review process.

A regulator complaint usually does not replace an appeal or pause an appeal deadline. Preserve every deadline while the complaint is pending.

Health warning: If waiting could seriously jeopardize your life, health, or ability to regain maximum function, ask the plan for an expedited appeal immediately. Have your prescriber explain the urgency in writing. Call 911 or seek emergency care when appropriate.

Rung 1 — Internal appeal

An internal appeal asks the insurer or plan to reconsider its own denial.

Your filing deadline

The denial notice must state the deadline and filing instructions. Under the federal ERISA claims rule, a claimant generally must receive at least 180 days after an adverse benefit determination to appeal.[2] State law, the policy, Medicare, Medicaid, or another coverage system may use a different deadline.

Use the deadline printed on your notice. If it conflicts with this guide, file by the earlier date and ask for written clarification.

The plan’s usual decision deadlines

For many employer health plans subject to the federal claims rule, an appeal decision is generally due within:[2]

  • 72 hours for an urgent-care appeal
  • 30 days for a pre-service appeal
  • 60 days for a post-service appeal

A pre-service claim is one that must be approved before receiving the service or medication. A post-service claim seeks payment or reimbursement after care was provided.

Different rules may apply to individual-market coverage, grandfathered plans, Medicare, Medicaid, governmental plans, church plans, and state-specific coverage. Check the denial notice and governing plan document.

What the internal appeal should do

Your appeal should:

  1. Identify the member, medication, prescriber, claim, and denial.
  2. Quote the exact denial reason.
  3. State what outcome you want.
  4. Address the denial criterion directly.
  5. Point to supporting records.
  6. Request the policy language and complete file.
  7. Ask for expedited handling if delay creates a serious health risk.
  8. Request a written decision explaining every reason for the outcome.

Rung 2 — External independent review

External review sends an eligible dispute to an independent review organization rather than another reviewer working for the plan.

Section 2719 of the Public Health Service Act requires applicable non-grandfathered coverage to provide internal appeals and external review.[4] The implementing regulation is 45 C.F.R. § 147.136.[3]

When external review may be available

External review commonly applies when the final denial involves:

  • Medical necessity
  • Health-care setting
  • Level of care
  • Effectiveness of a covered benefit
  • Experimental or investigational treatment
  • Another issue involving medical judgment
  • Rescission of coverage

Availability depends on the plan, the reason for denial, and whether a state or federal process applies.

A dispute about an explicit benefit exclusion, eligibility, premium payment, or other pure contract issue may fall outside external review unless deciding the dispute requires medical judgment. Ask the external-review administrator to make the eligibility determination rather than assuming you are ineligible.

Do you have to finish the internal appeal first?

Usually, yes. External review normally follows a final internal adverse benefit determination.

Exceptions may apply when:

  • The plan waives the internal appeal.
  • The plan fails to comply with applicable appeal requirements.
  • An expedited external review is allowed because waiting would create the required serious health risk.
  • The denial concerns certain ongoing or urgent treatment.

Procedural exceptions are fact-specific. Preserve the internal deadline unless the plan confirms a waiver in writing.

External-review filing deadline

Under the federal external-review process, a request generally must be filed within four months after the date you receive the adverse benefit determination or final internal adverse benefit determination.[3]

If the fourth month has no corresponding date, the deadline is the last day of that month under the federal rule.[3] State external-review deadlines and submission rules may differ. The final denial notice should identify the correct process.

External-review decision timelines

Under the federal external-review process:[3]

  • The plan generally completes preliminary review within five business days after receiving the request.
  • It generally sends written notice of preliminary eligibility within one business day after completing that review.
  • The independent review organization generally issues its decision within 45 days after receiving the external-review request.
  • An expedited external-review decision generally must be issued as quickly as the condition requires, but no later than 72 hours after the request is received.

State processes may use different timelines while still meeting federal minimum consumer-protection standards.

When to request expedited external review

Ask for expedited review if the applicable standard is met, including when waiting for the normal process could seriously jeopardize your life, health, or ability to regain maximum function. A prescriber should provide a written statement explaining the specific risk of delay.

An expedited request should not merely state that the medication is important. It should explain why the standard timeline creates the required health risk.

Where to file

Use the external-review instructions in the final denial notice.

Depending on the plan and state, the request may go to:

  • The state Department of Insurance
  • A state external-review office
  • The plan or insurer
  • A federally administered external-review process
  • A designated independent review organization

HealthCare.gov provides a starting point for external-review information.[5] The exact administrator for your plan and state must be confirmed from the final denial notice.

What the decision means

Under the federal process, the external-review decision is binding on the plan and claimant except to the extent other remedies are available under state or federal law.[3] If the reviewer reverses the denial, contact the plan immediately and ask it to confirm authorization, pharmacy routing, effective dates, and any remaining cost sharing in writing.

If external review upholds the denial, you may still have other options, including a regulator complaint, an ERISA benefits claim, or another legal remedy. Those options are plan- and fact-specific.

Rung 3 — State Department of Insurance complaint

A Department of Insurance complaint asks the state regulator to examine whether an insurer followed insurance law, policy terms, notice requirements, and required appeal procedures.

It is most useful when:

  • The denial notice omitted appeal rights or deadlines.
  • The insurer did not acknowledge or decide an appeal on time.
  • You cannot obtain the policy, criteria, or claim file.
  • The plan gave conflicting reasons for denial.
  • The insurer is not following an external-review decision.
  • The insurer applied the wrong policy, formulary, or plan year.
  • The insurer says no appeal or external-review right exists.
  • Calls and written submissions are being lost or ignored.
  • The insurer’s conduct appears inconsistent with state insurance requirements.

A regulator complaint is not a substitute for medical evidence. A Department of Insurance also may lack jurisdiction over a self-funded employer plan.

Complaint deadline

There is no single nationwide Department of Insurance complaint deadline. State rules and limitation periods vary.

File promptly. Continue every insurance appeal and external-review filing by its own deadline. Do not assume the complaint pauses or extends those deadlines.

Find the correct state regulator

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners maintains a directory of state insurance departments.[6]

Usually start with the insurance department in the state where the policy was issued. For employer coverage, that may differ from the state where you live. Ask the plan to identify the policy’s state of issue if it is unclear.

  1. An individual claim denial, which may be appealable;
  2. A plan-wide exclusion, which may limit medical-necessity arguments;

  4. An incorrect application of the new exclusion to an earlier claim; or

Special case: the plan changes its reason during the appeal

Medicare and Medicaid are different

Medicare

Medicare uses its own appeal system rather than the ACA state/federal external-review ladder described above.

For Medicare Part D, the path generally begins with a coverage determination, followed by redetermination and additional review levels.[7] The denial notice should state the filing deadline and whether an expedited request is available.

Do not send a Medicare Part D denial to a state ACA external-review program unless an official notice specifically directs you there.

Medicaid

Medicaid appeal and fair-hearing procedures vary by state and by whether coverage is administered through a managed-care organization. Follow the notice’s deadline, which may also affect whether benefits continue while an appeal is pending. Continued-benefit rules and deadlines are state- and program-specific.

Route 6: Compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide

The short version

Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. FDA does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. They can be legally compounded only under specific federal and state conditions.

A lower price does not make a source legitimate. A telehealth website, wellness clinic, or “research peptide” seller is not automatically a licensed pharmacy.

FDA source: Understanding the Risks of Compounded Drugs

How shortage status affects compounding

Federal law generally limits the compounding of products that are essentially copies of commercially available FDA-approved drugs. Different rules apply to state-licensed pharmacies operating under section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and outsourcing facilities operating under section 503B. Drug-shortage status can affect whether certain copies may be compounded.

FDA previously determined that the tirzepatide injection shortage was resolved and later determined that the semaglutide injection shortage was resolved. Temporary enforcement-discretion periods announced by FDA in connection with those shortage resolutions may have ended. Litigation or later FDA action may have changed the practical status.

Do not rely on a seller’s claim that “personalized dosing” or adding an ingredient automatically makes routine compounding lawful. Whether a prescription presents a legally significant change for an identified patient is fact-specific.

Check current primary sources:

  • FDA Drug Shortages Database
  • FDA: Compounding When Drugs Are on FDA’s Drug Shortages List
  • FDA: Concerns with Unapproved GLP-1 Drugs Used for Weight Loss
  • FDA 503A and 503B bulk drug-substance resources
  • Your state board of pharmacy

Semaglutide base versus salt forms

FDA has raised concerns about compounded products made with semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate. These salt forms are different active ingredients from the semaglutide base used in FDA-approved products, and FDA has stated it is not aware of a lawful basis for their use in compounding under applicable federal requirements except where a legal condition is satisfied.

Ask the pharmacy in writing:

“What exact active pharmaceutical ingredient is used: semaglutide base, semaglutide sodium, semaglutide acetate, or something else?”

If the seller will not answer, do not buy.

Dosing risks

FDA has reported adverse events, including some requiring hospitalization, associated with dosing errors involving compounded injectable semaglutide. Errors have involved confusion among milligrams, milliliters, “units,” vial concentrations, and syringe sizes.

FDA source: FDA alert on dosing errors associated with compounded injectable semaglutide

Before accepting a compounded prescription, get all of this in writing:

  • Drug name
  • Exact concentration in mg/mL
  • Prescribed dose in mg
  • Injection volume in mL
  • Syringe size
  • Syringe marking that corresponds to the prescribed dose
  • Dosing frequency
  • Storage instructions
  • Beyond-use date
  • Lot or batch number
  • What to do after a missed dose
  • Who to contact after a dosing error

“Units” alone are not enough unless the concentration, syringe, and corresponding volume are also clear.

How to vet a compounding source

Confirm the dispenser

Ask:

  1. What is the pharmacy’s legal name?
  2. In which state is it licensed?
  3. What is its license number?
  4. Is it a state-licensed 503A pharmacy or an FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facility?
  5. Does it hold the required license to ship into my state?
  6. Will the prescription label name the dispensing pharmacy?
  7. Will a pharmacist answer product and dosing questions?

Verify independently:

  • FDA Registered Outsourcing Facilities
  • FDA Compounding Inspections, Recalls, and Other Actions
  • Your state board of pharmacy
  • NABP Safe Pharmacy

FDA registration as a 503B outsourcing facility is not the same as FDA approval of a drug. State licensure is not proof that every product or claim is lawful.

Confirm the prescription process

A legitimate source should require a valid prescription based on an appropriate patient-prescriber relationship. Requirements vary by state and care setting.

Be cautious if the seller:

  • Ships without a prescription
  • Uses only a short questionnaire with no meaningful clinical review
  • Will not identify the prescriber or pharmacy
  • Sells “research use only” material for human injection
  • Calls a product “generic Ozempic,” “generic Wegovy,” or “generic Zepbound”
  • Guarantees a specific amount of weight loss
  • Refuses to state the active ingredient or concentration
  • Accepts only cryptocurrency, wire transfer, or payment apps
  • Ships medication with no pharmacy label
  • Provides a prefilled syringe with unclear concentration or dating
  • Claims FDA approval for a compounded version

There are no FDA-approved generic versions of Ozempic, Wegovy, or Zepbound as of the review date.

Ask about quality documentation

Ask the pharmacy:

  • Who manufactured the active pharmaceutical ingredient?
  • Is the ingredient from an FDA-registered establishment?
  • Does the pharmacy perform identity, potency, sterility, and endotoxin testing as applicable?
  • Is testing performed per batch or through another validated process?
  • Can it provide a certificate of analysis or a summary of testing?
  • Has the pharmacy or facility had recalls, warning letters, inspection findings, or disciplinary actions?
  • How is cold-chain shipping monitored?
  • What happens if the package arrives warm, damaged, or late?

A certificate of analysis does not by itself establish that a compounded drug is legal, sterile, safe, or effective.

“Personalized” compounded products

A seller may add vitamin B12, change the concentration, or offer a nonstandard dose and call the result “personalized.” That does not automatically establish:

  • A clinical need
  • Better effectiveness
  • Lower side-effect risk
  • FDA approval
  • Compliance with compounding law
  • Insurance coverage

Ask your prescriber to document the specific clinical reason for any variation from an available FDA-approved product.

Insurance and compounded products

Most health plans do not cover compounded GLP-1 products, but coverage varies. Paying for a compounded product usually does not prove that your plan must cover the branded medication.

Do not send a compounded-product receipt as if it were a receipt for Wegovy, Ozempic, or Zepbound. They are not the same product.

Denial-Letter Decoder

Turn insurer language into your next step

A denial letter can make a simple decision sound complicated. This decoder helps you identify:

  1. What was denied
  2. Why it was denied
  3. Whether the insurer wants more information, a different process, or no coverage at all
  4. Which appeal template to use
  5. What deadline applies

Do not rely on a code by itself. Insurers, pharmacy benefit managers, and medical plans do not all use the same codes. Read the full explanation next to the code and confirm its meaning with the plan.

Start with these five lines

Find and highlight the following information in your denial letter, Explanation of Benefits, or pharmacy rejection:

  • Service or drug denied: ______
  • Denial reason or code: ______
  • Rule or plan provision cited: ______
  • Appeal deadline: ______
  • Where the appeal must be sent: ______

Also look for the words “adverse benefit determination,” “coverage determination,” “appeal rights,” “grievance,” “exception,” or “external review.”

If the notice does not state the specific reason, clinical criteria, deadline, or appeal address, call the plan and request a complete written denial notice.

Core denial terms

Adverse benefit determination

A formal decision denying, reducing, terminating, or failing to provide or pay for a benefit. This can include a denial based on eligibility, medical necessity, experimental treatment, or a plan exclusion.

The notice should explain the reason for the decision and how to appeal. Requirements differ among employer plans, individual plans, Medicare, Medicaid, and other coverage.

Your move: Locate the appeal deadline and request every rule, guideline, record, and plan provision used in the decision.

Source: U.S. Department of Labor, Filing a Claim for Your Health Benefits:
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/filing-a-claim-for-your-health-benefits

Appeal

A request for the insurer or plan to reconsider a denial.

An internal appeal is reviewed through the plan’s own process. An external review is conducted outside the plan when the denial and plan type qualify.

Your move: Follow the filing instructions in the denial notice. Keep proof of delivery and a full copy of what you submitted.

Source: HealthCare.gov, How to appeal an insurance company decision:
https://www.healthcare.gov/appeal-insurance-company-decision/

Benefit exclusion

The plan document says a category of care or medication is not covered. This is different from a decision that treatment is not medically necessary.

Examples may include:

  • Drugs used for weight management
  • Compounded drugs
  • Cosmetic treatment
  • Certain over-the-counter products
  • Services outside the plan’s defined benefits

Your move: Ask for the exact exclusion and the full plan document or Evidence of Coverage. Check whether the exclusion applies to the drug itself, the diagnosis, or only one use of the drug.

A strong medical-necessity argument does not automatically override an explicit plan exclusion. Your appeal may instead focus on misclassification, inconsistent application, another covered indication, plan-document language, or whether the exclusion was properly disclosed.

Benefit maximum

The plan says you reached a limit on the amount, number, frequency, or duration it covers.

Your move: Ask what limit applies, how the plan calculated your usage, and whether an exception process exists.

Claim denial

A refusal to pay a submitted medical or pharmacy claim. A claim denial can happen after treatment or after a prescription is processed.

This is not always the same as a denial of prior authorization. You may need to correct a billing or eligibility issue rather than prove medical necessity.

Your move: Ask whether the plan denied:

  • The prescription itself
  • A prior-authorization request
  • A reimbursement claim
  • A specific quantity or dose
  • The pharmacy or prescriber
  • Your eligibility on the fill date

Clinical criteria

The insurer’s written rules for deciding when a drug or service is covered. Criteria may require documentation such as diagnosis, treatment history, lab results, contraindications, response to treatment, or participation in a specified program.

Your move: Request the exact clinical policy and version date used for your decision. Give it to the prescriber with the Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet.

Do not settle for “criteria not met.” Ask which numbered criterion was not met and what record was missing.

Continuation of therapy

A request to keep covering treatment you have already started. Continuation criteria may differ from criteria for a person starting the drug for the first time.

The insurer may ask for evidence of response, adherence, current diagnosis, updated measurements, or continued eligibility under the policy.

Your move: Ask for the plan’s current reauthorization or continuation-of-therapy criteria. Have the prescriber document the treatment timeline and what happened while you were taking the medication.

Coverage determination

In Medicare drug coverage, a coverage determination is the plan’s initial decision about whether a drug is covered and what you must pay. It may include a request for a formulary, tiering, quantity-limit, or step-therapy exception.

Your move: Use the Medicare plan’s coverage-determination process rather than a general customer-service complaint.

Source: Medicare.gov, Drug plan coverage rules:
https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/prescription-drugs-outpatient

Diagnosis not covered

The plan says the diagnosis submitted with the request does not qualify under its policy.

This can happen when:

  • The diagnosis code is missing or incorrect
  • The prescribed use is outside the plan’s covered criteria
  • The drug is covered for one condition but not another
  • The request does not document the condition clearly enough

Your move: Ask the prescriber to confirm the diagnosis submitted. Do not ask anyone to use an inaccurate diagnosis code. If the diagnosis is correct, request the policy that excludes or limits that use.

Dose exceeds plan limit

The requested strength, dose, number of units, or dosing frequency exceeds the plan’s standard allowance.

Your move: Confirm that the prescription was entered correctly. If the dose is intentional, ask the prescriber to explain why the standard limit is not appropriate and request a quantity-limit exception.

Drug utilization review / DUR

A safety or use check triggered during pharmacy processing. It can involve interactions, duplicate therapy, dose, timing, age, pregnancy, or another clinical issue.

A DUR message is not always a final coverage denial. A pharmacist or prescriber may be able to resolve it by reviewing the alert.

Your move: Ask the pharmacist to read the full rejection message and state whether the claim needs a pharmacist override, prescriber clarification, prior authorization, or appeal.

Eligibility denial

The plan says the patient was not covered on the date of service or was not eligible for the benefit.

Your move: Check premium payments, enrollment dates, employer records, dependent status, coordination of benefits, and the plan effective date. Send proof of active coverage if the insurer’s record is wrong.

This is usually an enrollment or records problem, not a medical-necessity problem.

Evidence of Coverage / EOC

A document describing a plan’s covered benefits, exclusions, limits, and appeal process. Medicare Advantage and Part D plans commonly use this name.

Your move: Search the document for:

  • The drug name
  • “Weight loss”
  • “Obesity”
  • “Anti-obesity”
  • “Prior authorization”
  • “Step therapy”
  • “Quantity limit”
  • “Exclusions”
  • “Appeals”
  • “Coverage determination”

Exception request

A request that the plan make an exception to a normal coverage rule.

Common examples include:

  • Formulary exception
  • Tiering exception
  • Step-therapy exception
  • Quantity-limit exception
  • Network exception

An exception is not automatic. The plan will usually require a clinical explanation from the prescriber.

Your move: Name the exact exception you are requesting. “Please cover this medication” is less precise than “I request a formulary exception and waiver of step therapy.”

Experimental or investigational

The insurer says the requested use does not meet its standard for established treatment.

Your move: Request the plan’s definition, the specific policy section, the evidence reviewed, and the qualifications of the reviewer. Ask the prescriber to address the policy directly with clinical evidence relevant to your diagnosis.

Do not assume that FDA approval for one use requires a plan to cover every use.

External review

A review by an independent organization outside the health plan. Eligibility depends on the type of plan, the denial reason, and applicable federal or state rules.

Not every denial qualifies. Pure contract exclusions and eligibility disputes may be treated differently from medical-necessity or clinical denials.

Your move: Read the external-review section of the denial notice and use the Escalation Ladder. Do not miss the filing deadline while waiting for another response.

Source: HealthCare.gov, How to appeal an insurance company decision:
https://www.healthcare.gov/appeal-insurance-company-decision/

Final adverse benefit determination

The plan’s final decision after its internal appeal process. This notice may trigger a right to request external review or move to another appeal level.

Your move: Save the notice and envelope. Record the date received. Follow the next-level instructions stated in the notice.

Formulary

The list of prescription drugs a plan covers, often organized into cost-sharing tiers.

A drug’s presence on the formulary does not mean it is covered in every situation. Prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, diagnosis rules, or exclusions may still apply.

Your move: Check the formulary for the exact drug, strength, dosage form, and any restriction symbols.

Formulary exception

A request to cover a drug that is not on the plan’s formulary.

The prescriber may need to explain why covered alternatives would not be as effective, have failed, or would cause adverse effects.

Your move: Ask the plan for its exception form and criteria. Attach the medication history and the prescriber’s explanation.

Grievance

A complaint about service, access, conduct, delays, or administration. A grievance is often separate from an appeal of a coverage denial.

Examples can include:

  • Repeated failure to send the denial notice
  • Excessive hold times or disconnected calls
  • Failure to provide accessible information
  • Delay in processing
  • Conduct by plan staff

Your move: If you want the coverage decision changed, file the appeal or exception request. A grievance alone may not preserve your appeal rights.

Independent review organization / IRO

An outside entity that reviews certain eligible coverage disputes. Other notices may use terms such as independent review entity or external review organization.

Your move: Follow the instructions in the final denial. Submit the complete internal-appeal file plus any new information allowed by the process.

Medical necessity

A plan standard used to decide whether care is appropriate and covered under its policy. The exact definition comes from the plan document and clinical policy.

“Your doctor prescribed it” is important, but it may not answer every requirement in the insurer’s medical-necessity criteria.

Your move: Ask for:

  • The plan’s definition of medical necessity
  • The clinical policy used
  • Every criterion the plan says was not met
  • The reviewer’s specialty
  • The records reviewed
  • Any missing information

Then have the prescriber respond criterion by criterion.

Missing information / insufficient documentation

The insurer says the request did not contain enough information to make or approve the decision.

Common missing items can include:

  • Diagnosis
  • Clinical notes
  • Treatment history
  • Dates and outcomes of previous medication trials
  • Current or historical measurements
  • Relevant laboratory results
  • Contraindications or intolerance
  • Dose and duration requested

Your move: Ask for a complete list of missing items. Do not resend the same incomplete request without checking the criteria.

Network exception

A request to use an out-of-network prescriber, pharmacy, or facility at in-network coverage terms because an adequate in-network option is unavailable or inappropriate.

Your move: Ask what in-network alternative the plan says is available. Document unsuccessful calls, distance, wait times, accessibility issues, and whether the listed provider actually offers the needed service.

Non-covered benefit

The plan says the requested item falls outside the benefits promised by the contract.

This phrase can mean a true exclusion, but it can also appear when the drug, diagnosis, provider, or submission does not match the plan’s rules.

Your move: Ask the plan to identify the exact plan section that makes the item non-covered.

Non-formulary

The drug is not included on the plan’s covered-drug list.

Your move: Ask for:

  1. The covered alternatives
  2. The formulary-exception process
  3. Any step-therapy requirements
  4. The criteria the prescriber must address

Off-label use

Use of an FDA-approved drug for a condition, population, dose, or treatment approach not included in the FDA-approved labeling.

Off-label prescribing can be lawful, but insurance coverage is a separate question. Coverage may depend on the plan, benefit type, diagnosis, evidence, and applicable coverage rules.

Your move: Ask for the policy governing the requested use. Have the prescriber address the diagnosis, evidence, treatment history, and why covered alternatives are not appropriate.

Source: FDA, Understanding Unapproved Use of Approved Drugs “Off Label”:
https://www.fda.gov/patients/learn-about-expanded-access-and-other-treatment-options/understanding-unapproved-use-approved-drugs-label

Out-of-network

The prescriber, pharmacy, facility, or supplier does not have a contract with the plan for the relevant service.

Your move: Confirm which participant is out of network. It may be the pharmacy rather than the prescriber. Ask for an in-network option or the network-exception procedure.

Plan exclusion

A contract term that removes a category of benefits from coverage.

For GLP-1 requests, the wording matters. A plan may refer to:

  • Weight-loss drugs
  • Anti-obesity medications
  • Drugs used solely for weight reduction
  • Lifestyle or obesity services
  • Compounded products
  • Drugs not approved for the submitted diagnosis

Your move: Obtain the complete exclusion, including definitions and exceptions. Do not rely on a customer-service summary.

Plan limitation exceeded

A broad message indicating that the claim conflicts with a limit. It may involve quantity, frequency, duration, age, refill timing, cost, or another plan rule.

Your move: Ask the pharmacist or insurer to identify the exact limit and the procedure for requesting an exception.

Precertification

Another term for prior authorization. The plan requires approval before covering the requested treatment or claim.

Your move: Confirm whether the request was never submitted, is still pending, was closed for missing information, or was denied on the merits.

Prior authorization / PA

Approval required before the plan will cover a drug or service under specified conditions.

A PA requirement is not itself proof that the drug is excluded. It means the plan wants information reviewed before approval.

Your move: Use the Doctor’s Office Prior-Auth Worksheet. Confirm that the prescriber sent the request to the correct medical or pharmacy-benefit administrator.

Reauthorization

A new review required after an existing approval period ends.

Your move: Start before the approval expires. Ask for continuation criteria and document treatment response, interruptions, side effects, and any current measurements the policy requires.

Reconsideration

A later appeal stage in some programs. In Medicare Part D, a reconsideration generally follows a plan-level redetermination and is handled by an independent review entity. Terminology and procedures depend on the benefit involved.

Your move: Use the exact form and deadline named in the notice.

Source: Medicare.gov, Drug coverage appeals:
https://www.medicare.gov/providers-services/claims-appeals-complaints/appeals/drug-coverage

Redetermination

In Medicare Part D, the first appeal of an unfavorable coverage determination is generally called a redetermination.

Your move: Follow the plan’s redetermination instructions and ask whether an expedited decision is available if delay could seriously jeopardize health.

Source: Medicare.gov, Drug coverage appeals:
https://www.medicare.gov/providers-services/claims-appeals-complaints/appeals/drug-coverage

Refill too soon

The plan believes the prescription is being refilled before the covered supply should have run out.

Possible reasons include:

  • The pharmacy entered the wrong days’ supply
  • The dose changed
  • Medication was lost or damaged
  • Travel requires an early fill
  • A replacement device is needed
  • Another pharmacy already processed the claim

Your move: Ask the pharmacist for the next covered fill date and whether a vacation, lost-medication, dose-change, or other override is available.

Step therapy / fail first

A rule requiring you to try one or more preferred treatments before the plan will cover the requested drug.

A previous treatment trial may not appear in the insurer’s claim history, especially if it occurred under another plan, was paid in cash, or was documented only in medical records.

Your move: Build a dated list of previous treatments, doses, duration, results, adverse effects, and reasons each option was stopped or could not be used.

Summary Plan Description / SPD

A document explaining benefits and rights under many employer-sponsored health plans.

Your move: Ask HR or the plan administrator for the current SPD, applicable amendments, and any separate pharmacy-benefit document. Also ask whether the plan is fully insured or self-funded.

For many private-sector employer plans, the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employee Benefits Security Administration provides claims and appeals guidance.

Source: U.S. Department of Labor, Filing a Claim for Your Health Benefits:
https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/filing-a-claim-for-your-health-benefits

Tiering exception

A request to place a covered drug on a lower cost-sharing tier. This addresses what you pay, not necessarily whether the drug is covered at all.

Your move: Confirm that the plan permits tiering exceptions for the drug and tier involved. Some plan designs do not allow them in every situation.

Quantity limit / QL

A cap on the amount of a drug covered during a defined period.

The limit can involve:

  • Number of pens, vials, or tablets
  • Dose per day
  • Fills per month
  • Days’ supply
  • Dose-escalation schedule

Your move: Compare the written prescription with the pharmacy claim. If the request is correct, ask for a quantity-limit exception.

GLP-1-specific phrases

“Anti-obesity medication excluded”

The plan appears to exclude medications used to treat obesity or for weight reduction.

Check before accepting the denial:

  • What exact exclusion applies?
  • Is the denial based on the drug, the diagnosis, or the submitted use?
  • Does the plan cover the drug for any other indication?
  • Was the correct diagnosis submitted?
  • Is there an exception, medical-benefit pathway, or employer carve-in?
  • Did the plan change during the year, and what notice was provided?

Use the Plan-Exclusion Appeal Letter.

“A1C criteria not met”

The plan may be evaluating the request under diabetes-specific criteria.

Your move: Ask for the exact required measurement, date range, diagnosis rule, and policy version. Have the prescriber verify that the request was submitted for the intended diagnosis and benefit pathway.

Do not substitute a diagnosis solely to obtain coverage.

“BMI criteria not met”

The plan says its required body-mass-index threshold or related documentation was not established.

This may mean the number itself did not qualify, or that the plan did not receive an acceptable dated record.

Your move: Ask whether the plan requires current BMI, historical BMI, a qualifying comorbidity, or records from before treatment began. Have the prescriber document the relevant history accurately.

“Lifestyle-modification requirement not met”

The plan says it requires documentation of a diet, activity, behavioral, or structured weight-management effort.

Your move: Ask what counts, how long participation must last, and what records are accepted. Do not assume the plan requires a paid commercial program unless the written criteria say so.

“No documented trial of preferred agent”

The plan cannot find proof that a required alternative was tried.

Your move: Provide dates, dose, duration, outcome, adverse effects, and the pharmacy or prescriber involved. If the alternative is unsafe or inappropriate, the prescriber should explain why.

“Product not covered for weight loss”

The plan may cover the drug in some situations but not for the diagnosis or use submitted.

Your move: Request the exact policy and exclusion. Confirm the diagnosis and intended use submitted by the prescriber.

“Compounded drug not covered”

The plan may exclude compounded preparations, individual ingredients, nonparticipating compounding pharmacies, or products without an eligible billing identifier.

Insurance coverage and federal permission to compound are separate issues. A compounded product is not automatically covered because a commercially manufactured drug is unavailable. Federal and state rules can change.

Your move: Ask whether the denial is based on:

  • A plan exclusion
  • The pharmacy’s network status
  • The product identifier
  • Ingredient coverage
  • FDA approval status
  • Medical necessity
  • Compounding-law requirements
  • A claim-submission error

Use the Compounded-Version Denial Letter and the Cost-Bridge Guide.

Source: FDA, Compounding and the FDA: Questions and Answers:
https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers

“Not FDA approved”

This can mean different things:

  • The drug itself is not FDA approved
  • The compounded version is not FDA approved
  • The requested use is not in the approved labeling
  • The submitted product identifier does not match a covered product

Your move: Ask which meaning applies. Do not let the plan use one phrase to blur separate issues.

“Maintenance dose not supported”

The plan says the requested ongoing dose does not match its policy, labeling rule, or submitted treatment history.

Your move: Check the prescription, dose-escalation record, prior fills, missed doses, and the insurer’s dosing criteria. Ask the prescriber to explain the selected dose.

“New start” versus “continuation”

The plan may be treating you as a new patient even if you previously used the medication.

This can happen after:

  • A plan change
  • A lapse in coverage
  • A treatment interruption
  • Cash-paid fills
  • Use of a different pharmacy
  • A switch between products

Your move: Submit dated records showing prior use and ask which definition of “continuation” the plan applies.

Common pharmacy rejection codes

Pharmacy point-of-sale messages often use NCPDP reject codes. The number is only a starting point. The full response can include additional text, fields, and plan-specific instructions.

Ask the pharmacist to print or read the complete rejection. Code wording and implementation should be checked against the current transaction standard and the plan’s response.

Code commonly shown Common label Plain-English meaning First action

75 Prior authorization required Approval is required before the claim can be paid. Confirm whether a PA has been submitted and which administrator handles it.
76 Plan limitations exceeded A plan limit was triggered. Ask whether the limit concerns quantity, dose, frequency, duration, age, or another rule.

Names that are not denial reasons

These items may appear near the rejection but do not, by themselves, explain why coverage was denied.

BIN

A routing number used to send a pharmacy claim to the correct processor.

PCN

A pharmacy-processing identifier used with the BIN.

Group or RXGRP

An identifier connecting the claim to a particular employer or insurance benefit group.

Member ID

The number identifying the covered person.

NDC

The National Drug Code submitted for the drug product. A mismatch involving the product, package, or identifier can affect claim processing.

PBM

Pharmacy benefit manager. A PBM may operate the prescription benefit, formulary, pharmacy network, prior-authorization process, or pharmacy claims system for the insurer or employer plan.

Reference number

The plan’s tracking number for a call, authorization, claim, or appeal. Save it, but do not assume it proves that an appeal was filed.

Before you appeal

Collect these documents:

  • The complete denial notice
  • The pharmacy rejection printout, if available
  • The prescription and requested dose
  • The plan formulary
  • The applicable clinical policy
  • The plan document, Evidence of Coverage, or Summary Plan Description
  • The prior-authorization submission
  • Relevant clinical records
  • Previous medication history
  • Proof of active coverage
  • Notes and reference numbers from every call

Then match the denial to the correct letter:

  1. Prior authorization required or missing
    Use the Prior-Authorization Appeal Letter.

  2. Not medically necessary, off-label, experimental, or criteria not met
    Use the Medical-Necessity Appeal Letter.

  3. Weight-loss or drug-category exclusion
    Use the Plan-Exclusion Appeal Letter.

  4. Step therapy, quantity limit, dose limit, or refill restriction
    Use the Quantity-Limit/Step-Therapy Appeal Letter.

  5. Compounded product excluded or rejected
    Use the Compounded-Version Denial Letter.

If more than one reason appears, address each reason separately. A plan can uphold a denial based on a second reason even after you resolve the first.

Keep the deadline in view

The deadline printed in your notice controls. Appeal periods differ by plan type and review stage.

Do not wait for the insurer, employer, pharmacy, or doctor’s office to call you back if the deadline is approaching. File what you have, state that supporting records are being requested, and ask whether additional material can be submitted afterward.

For urgent health situations, ask whether an expedited review is available. The plan—not this glossary—determines whether the request meets its expedited-review standard.
